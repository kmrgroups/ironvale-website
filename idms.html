Training Plan vs Actual

One record per session, planned first and completed afterwards. A session cannot be marked done without a date and somebody who attended — a training record with no attendees is the one an auditor asks about. The plan-against-actual figure on the HR dashboard is counted from these records, so nothing has to be typed there.

Training Effectiveness

Whether the training changed anything on the shop floor. The level each attendee held on the day is read from the skill matrix history, and the level they hold now is read from the same place — so "improved" is something the records show, not something the trainer says. Effectiveness cannot be judged the same week: a session is only offered here once 30 days have passed, because the question is whether it stuck, not whether it was enjoyed.

Capacity Plan

Hours needed against hours available, machine by machine and month by month. The production plan answers whether there is enough capacity overall; this answers when it runs out, which is the question that decides whether you buy a machine or work a third shift. Demand comes from the open orders and the routing cycle times; nothing is estimated.

What each machine can do

Shifts, hours and working days are per machine, because they are not the same for every machine in a real works. Availability is what is left after planned maintenance, setting and the rest — set it from the actual figure on the production dashboard rather than from 100%, or this plan will promise hours that do not exist. Changes save as you make them.

Machine Loading Plan

The open orders put onto the machines in date order, one after another, until each machine's day is full — so an order that cannot be started until the machine ahead of it is clear shows the date it will actually finish, not the date somebody hoped for. Two assumptions worth knowing: an operation finishes before the next one on the same order starts (no overlapping batches), and earliest due date is loaded first. It is a projection from today's order book, not a commitment.

Bill of Materials

What one piece of this part consumes. Materials come from the material master, not free text, so what is consumed here and what was received on a goods receipt are the same thing and can be netted off. Rates are read from the master every time this is opened — they are not copied in, so a rate revision does not leave old bills quoting a price nobody charges any more.

Lines

Raw Material Stock

Nothing on this screen is typed except the opening balance. Stock is opening + accepted receipts − what production consumed, worked out from the goods receipts, the inward inspections and the production bookings against each part's bill of materials. Material that has been received but not yet inspected is shown separately and is not counted as stock, because it is not yours to use until it passes.

Control Charts

Drawn from the readings the operators already record on the self-inspection sheets — nothing is entered twice. Each check records one piece, so the chart is an individuals and moving range chart; an X̄–R chart would need several pieces per check and drawing one from single readings would put limits on the page that mean nothing.

PPAP submission

The eighteen elements, per AIAG. What this system already holds is ticked automatically and shows the document it found — the rest you record as on file, not applicable, or leave outstanding. The warrant cannot be signed while anything required at the chosen level is outstanding.

Elements

Part Submission Warrant

Signing the warrant is a declaration to your customer that the parts meet their requirements and were made at the production rate. It is a statement someone is answerable for.

CNC Programme

Read this before you use anything on this screen. A drafted programme is a starting point for a programmer, not a programme to run. It has not been simulated, the offsets and work coordinates are assumptions, and no machine here knows your fixtures. Post it to a control without proving it out and you will crash a spindle. Every file this screen produces carries that warning inside it, and it cannot be marked proven until someone puts their name to a dry run.

Programme

Prove-out record

Filled in after the programme has been run in single block with feed override down and the first part measured. Until then the programme stays marked unproven wherever it appears.

Control Plan

Built from the PFMEA, the routing and the dimensions, so every control answers a failure mode that was actually ranked. An auditor cross-checks the two documents against each other — generating the control plan from anything else is how they come apart.

Control plan header (AIAG)

These fields print in the header block of the form, as required by the AIAG Control Plan format. They are not generated — enter them once per part and they are kept with the document.

Control Plan

Process FMEA

Drafted by AI from the routing and the dimensions already on the part — every line is tied to a real operation and a real characteristic. Severity, occurrence and detection are starting points for your CFT to argue with, not answers. Nothing here is approved until a named team signs it.

PFMEA header (AIAG)

These fields print in the header block of the form, as required by the AIAG FMEA-4 format. They are not generated — enter them once per part and they are kept with the document.

PFMEA

Cross-functional team sign-off

A PFMEA is a team judgement, not a document one person produces. Record who reviewed it — the printed copy shows this, and until it is filled the print is stamped DRAFT.

Process Flow Diagram

Drawn from the routing — it is not typed separately, so it can never disagree with the operations you entered. Standard flowcharting symbols are used throughout: a box is an operation, a diamond is an inspection, and an inverted triangle is storage — the legend prints with the diagram.
